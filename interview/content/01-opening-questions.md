# The Opening Questions
@short: Opening Questions
@subtitle: The behavioural round, in words you can actually say
@tier: foundation
@prereq: none
@blurb: These are not technical questions and they are not small talk either. They are testing three things: can you hold a conversation, do you own your work, and will you be difficult to sit next to. Keep every answer under ninety seconds, and let them ask the follow-up.
@objectives:
- Open with a 60-second summary that invites the right follow-up
- Tell a failure story that ends in a fix, not an excuse
- Name a real weakness and the correction you made
- Close the interview by asking something that shows interest

## Who you are

#### Q1.1 — Tell me about yourself.
Say this: "I'm an AI/ML engineer, and I'm doing my M.Sc. in Data Science and
AI at BITS Pilani. My last role was an internship at Infosys, where I built
SafeSight — a workplace safety system. A camera model spots workers who
aren't wearing the right safety gear, and a second part looks up which safety
rule they've broken and shows it to the supervisor. Outside that I've built a
document question-answering tool, a fire detector, and a customer-churn
pipeline that's deployed and running. I'm looking for a job where I own a
model from training all the way to production."

That's about forty seconds. It gives them four things to ask about, and the
one they'll pick is SafeSight — which is what you want.

#### Q1.2 — Why AI/ML?
Don't be romantic about it. Say: "Because you can tell whether you were
right. You ship a model, the number tells you if it worked, and you can't
argue with it. Computer vision got me first — you can literally watch the
model succeed or fail frame by frame."

#### Q1.3 — Walk me through your resume.
Go in order and keep it to two minutes. Bachelor's in IT at Thakur, then the
AI/ML programme at IIT Hyderabad, then the M.Sc. at BITS, then the Infosys
internship. End on the internship — that's where you want them to dig.

#### Q1.4 — What is your greatest strength?
Pick one and prove it with a fact. "I finish things. Every project I've built
has a Dockerfile and a running endpoint, not just a notebook that worked once
on my laptop."

#### Q1.5 — What is your greatest weakness?
Name a real one and say what you did about it. "I used to spend all my time
on the model and treat deployment as something I'd sort out later. On the
churn project I made myself start with the Dockerfile and the pipeline
instead. It's still the part I have to be deliberate about."

Never say "I'm a perfectionist." Everyone has heard it and nobody believes it.

#### Q1.6 — Why should we hire you?
"I've already built the kind of system you're hiring for — a detection model,
a retrieval layer, an API, a dashboard, and a container to run it in. I'll
need to learn your domain, but not your stack."

#### Q1.7 — Tell me about a time you failed.
Use a real one with a fix attached. The PPE detector is good: the small items
like goggles were being missed. Say: "At first I assumed the model was too
small. When I actually looked at the data, there were far fewer goggle
examples than helmet examples — it was a data problem, not a model problem.
I rebalanced the dataset and it improved."

That story is strong because it shows you checked instead of guessing.

#### Q1.8 — Tell me about a conflict with a teammate.
Keep it about the work, never about the person. "We disagreed on which
approach to take. Rather than argue it out, I suggested we measure both and
let the numbers decide. They did."

Never tell a story where the other person is just an idiot. The interviewer
will assume you'll say the same about them.

#### Q1.9 — How do you handle criticism of your work?
"In a code review I'd much rather be corrected than ship something broken."
Then give one real example of feedback you acted on.

#### Q1.10 — Describe a time you worked under a tight deadline.
Use the internship. Talk about what you **cut**, not how hard you worked.
Deciding what not to build is the skill they're testing. "I dropped the
night-time footage work and shipped the daytime version first, because that
covered most of the shift patterns."

#### Q1.11 — How do you work in a team?
Be concrete. Don't say "I'm a good communicator" — everybody says that. Say:
"I keep work on its own branch and open a pull request early, while it's
still small enough for someone to actually read."

#### Q1.12 — Where do you see yourself in five years?
"Owning a machine learning system in production end to end, and helping
juniors with the deployment side — because that's the part most people pick
up last."

#### Q1.13 — Why are you leaving Infosys?
Stay neutral and look forward. "It was a fixed-term internship. I'm looking
for a role where I own a system for longer than three months."

Never criticise them. It tells the interviewer what you'll say about this
company later.

#### Q1.14 — What do you know about our company?
This is homework you cannot skip. Name their product, one recent launch, and
one guess about where machine learning sits in it. Then turn it into a
question: "I'd guess the recommendation side is where most of your ML effort
goes — is that right?"

Turning it into a question is the trick. It changes an exam into a
conversation.

## Practical questions

#### Q1.15 — What are your salary expectations?
Give a range you've actually researched for Mumbai at your level, and say
you're flexible. "Based on what I've seen for AI/ML roles here at my level,
I'm looking at X to Y. Honestly I care more about how much I'd own than the
exact number."

Never say "whatever you offer." It reads as having no idea what you're worth.

#### Q1.16 — How do you keep up with the field?
Name real things or skip the question. Papers you've actually read, the
Ultralytics release notes, the LangGraph changelog. "I follow AI news" means
nothing and they know it.

#### Q1.17 — Are you comfortable relocating?
Your resume says you're open to it, so say yes plainly. Contradicting your
own resume is an instant credibility problem.

#### Q1.18 — You're still doing your M.Sc. How will you manage both?
Have a concrete plan ready. "The course is structured so the work sits
outside office hours, and I built all four of my projects while studying —
so it's not theoretical."

#### Q1.19 — What's your biggest technical achievement?
Pick one and go deep. SafeSight is the strongest: it's the only one with real
users, a real deployment, and a measured number attached to it.

#### Q1.20 — Do you prefer research or engineering?
For most hiring managers, the honest engineering answer wins. "Engineering. I
like reading the paper, but what I actually enjoy is getting it to run on a
live video feed without falling over."

#### Q1.21 — What would you do differently on your last project?
This is testing self-awareness, so give a real answer. "I'd have built the
evaluation set before training anything, so every run was comparable from day
one. I ended up with numbers I couldn't properly compare."

#### Q1.22 — Do you have any questions for us?
Always yes. Chapter 19 has the list. Turning up with no questions reads as no
interest, and it's the easiest thing in the whole interview to get right.

:::practice Before the interview
Write out your answer to Q1.1 and say it out loud, timed. If it runs past
sixty seconds, cut it. Then do the same for Q1.7 and Q1.5 — the failure and
the weakness — because those are the two people improvise badly and regret.
:::

:::recap
- Lead with a forty-second summary that gives them four things to ask about.
- Every story needs a fix attached, not just a problem.
- Talk about what you cut under deadline, not how hard you worked.
- Never criticise a past employer or a teammate.
- Research the company. One specific fact turns the question into a
  conversation.
- Always have questions for them.
:::
