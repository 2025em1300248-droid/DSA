# Communicating About Models
@short: Communication
@subtitle: The skill that is scored lowest and separates senior from mid-level
@tier: core
@prereq: none
@blurb: An engineer who can explain a model's behaviour to a sceptical non-specialist, scope a problem with a stakeholder, and report a negative result without defensiveness is worth considerably more than one who cannot --- and the gap is larger than any technical gap at the same level. This chapter is about those three conversations.
@objectives:
- Translate metrics into the currency of the decision they inform
- Explain uncertainty without either hedging or overclaiming
- Scope a problem before anyone writes code
- Deliver a negative result well
- Write a document that survives being read without you present

## Translate the metric

A metric is meaningless to the person who must act on it until it is expressed
in their units.

| Technical statement | What they need to hear |
|---|---|
| "AUC improved from 0.84 to 0.87" | "We catch 120 more fraud cases a month at the same review load" |
| "Recall at the chosen threshold is 0.72" | "We catch about seven in ten; three in ten still get through" |
| "The p95 latency is 340 ms" | "One request in twenty takes over a third of a second" |
| "ECE is 0.03" | "When it says 80% confident, it is right about 80% of the time" |
| "The model is 94% accurate" | "About one in seventeen cases will be wrong --- here is what that looks like" |

@tbl: The right-hand column is not dumbed down; it is more precise about what will actually happen. The conversion requires knowing the volume, the cost of each error type and the operating threshold --- which is why Chapter 20 comes before this one.

:::insight Always give the error in absolute numbers
"94% accurate" sounds excellent. "Of the 50,000 claims we process each
month, about 3,000 will be classified wrongly" prompts the right next
question, which is *which* 3,000 and what happens to them.

Percentages flatter; counts inform. Lead with the count whenever the volume is
known, and follow with what the errors look like in practice.
:::

## Explain uncertainty without hedging

:::checklist Four sentences that work
**The estimate, with its range.** "Our best estimate is a 3-point
improvement, and the data is consistent with anywhere between 1 and 5."

**What would change your mind.** "If the new model underperforms on
enterprise accounts, we would see it in the canary within two days."

**The limit of what you measured.** "This is measured on last quarter's
traffic. If the product mix shifts significantly, I would want to re-measure."

**Your recommendation anyway.** "On balance I would ship it, with the canary
gate set at 2% and a review on Thursday."

The fourth is what distinguishes useful uncertainty from unhelpful hedging.
Stakeholders are not asking you to be certain; they are asking you to be
clear about what you know, and then to recommend something.
:::

:::pitfall Three failure modes, all common
**False precision.** "The model is 87.3% accurate" implies a precision the
sample size does not support. Say "about 87%, give or take two points".

**Defensive hedging.** Qualifying every statement until nothing has been said.
The listener concludes you do not know, and discounts everything.

**Burying the lede.** Opening with methodology. Decision-makers read the first
two sentences carefully and skim the rest; the conclusion goes first.
:::

## Scoping, before anyone writes code

@fig: scoping_questions | 168 | The scoping conversation. Each question eliminates a class of failure, and the order matters: a project that cannot answer the first three should not start, however good the data is.

:::checklist The seven questions
1. **What decision does this change?** If no decision changes, the project has
   no value regardless of accuracy. Surprisingly often the honest answer is
   "none".
2. **What happens today?** The current process is your baseline and often
   already good. You must beat it, not beat zero.
3. **What does an error cost?** Both directions, in the currency of the
   problem. This sets the threshold (Chapter 20) and often the whole design.
4. **How accurate does it have to be to be worth using?** Agree this *before*
   building. "As accurate as possible" is not an answer and makes success
   undefinable.
5. **What data exists, and was it generated by the current process?** If
   humans currently decide, your labels encode their decisions, including
   their biases --- and you are modelling the process, not the truth.
6. **Who acts on the output, and will they trust it?** A model nobody uses
   delivers nothing. Involve them now, not at launch.
7. **What is the fallback when it fails?** It will. Design the degraded mode
   before the model, not after the incident.
:::

:::warning The highest-value sentence you can say
*"I do not think machine learning is the right tool for this."*

Reach for it when: the rules are known and stable (write the rules); there are
fewer than a few hundred labelled examples; no decision actually changes; the
required accuracy is unachievable given the data's noise floor; or nobody can
say what an error costs.

Saying this costs you a project and buys you lasting credibility. It is scored
lowest on every self-assessment and is one of the two clearest markers of
seniority --- the other being the ability to explain a decision to the person
affected by it.
:::

## Delivering a negative result

```
Subject: Churn model — recommend not shipping

We built it, it works, and I do not recommend deploying it.

  Model AUC 0.71  [0.68, 0.74]
  Current heuristic AUC 0.69  [0.66, 0.72]
  Paired difference +0.02  [-0.01, +0.05]  (n = 2,100, p = 0.19)

The improvement is inside the noise. At our volumes it would mean
roughly 40 more correct flags a month against 1,200 reviews — well
below the 200 we agreed would make it worth the operational cost.

What we learned that is worth having:
  - The churn signal lives almost entirely in support-ticket history,
    which we do not currently join into the feature pipeline.
  - Our labels are 6 weeks stale; that alone caps achievable AUC
    at roughly 0.78 by my estimate.

What I would do instead:
  1. Join support tickets into the feature store (2 weeks, reusable).
  2. Shorten the label delay to 2 weeks (needs a decision from Ops).
  3. Revisit this in Q3 with both in place.

Happy to walk through any of it.
```

:::insight Why this is a good outcome, presented well
It leads with the recommendation. It gives the number *with an interval* and
in business units. It states the pre-agreed bar and shows the result against
it. It extracts the two durable findings. And it proposes next steps rather
than ending on failure.

A team that can deliver this well runs more experiments, because a negative
result stops being a career risk. A team that cannot quietly stops testing
things that might fail --- which is the only way to be wrong slowly and
expensively.
:::

## Writing

```
# One-page model summary                      [the only page most will read]

## Recommendation
Ship to 5% canary on Monday. Full rollout Thursday if the gate holds.

## What it does
Scores each transaction 0-1 for fraud risk. Above 0.029 goes to review.
Threshold set from the cost matrix agreed with Risk on 2026-02-11.

## Expected effect
  +120 fraud cases caught per month (±35)
  +400 reviews per month (within the team's 8,000 capacity)
  Net expected saving: £46,000/month  [£28k, £61k]

## What it does not do
Does not assess chargeback risk. Does not work for the new
marketplace flow (no training data yet). Not evaluated below £5.

## Risks and the fallback
If the review queue exceeds capacity we raise the threshold to 0.05,
which costs about 30 catches a month. One command, documented in the
runbook. If the model fails entirely we revert to the rules engine.

## How we will know it is working
Daily: review volume, score distribution, p95 latency.
Weekly: caught-fraud rate against the control group.
```

:::checklist The sections that make a document survive without you
- **Recommendation first**, in one sentence.
- **Numbers in business units**, with intervals.
- **What it does not do** --- the section that buys the most trust, because
  nobody expects it.
- **Risks and the fallback**, concretely.
- **How success will be measured**, agreed in advance.
:::

:::practice The task
(a) Take your last model's metrics and write the five-row translation table
for your stakeholders. (b) Write the one-page summary above for a real
system. (c) Run the seven scoping questions on a project currently in flight
--- which could you not answer? (d) Explain one prediction to someone outside
your field and note exactly where they stopped following. (e) Write the
negative-result message for an experiment that did not work. (f) Find a
project in your organisation that should not be using ML and make the case.

**You have this skill when** a stakeholder can read your one-pager, make the
decision, and brief their own manager on it without calling you.
:::

:::exercise
1. Convert three of your metrics into business units. What did you need to
   know that you did not have?
2. Rewrite a technical summary of yours to lead with the recommendation.
   Compare the two.
3. † Explain a model decision to someone outside the field. Record where they
   lost the thread and rewrite that part.
4. Run the seven scoping questions on a live project and report which went
   unanswered.
5. Write the "what it does not do" section for a system of yours. How long is
   it? Should it be longer?
6. † Take an experiment that failed and write it up as a positive
   contribution. What did it actually establish?
7. Identify a project that should not use ML, and write the one-paragraph case
   for the alternative.
:::

:::recap
- Translate every metric into the currency of the decision, and give errors as
  counts rather than percentages.
- Express uncertainty in four sentences: the range, what would change your
  mind, the limit of what you measured, and your recommendation anyway.
- Avoid false precision, defensive hedging and burying the lede.
- Scope with seven questions; a project that cannot answer the first three
  should not start.
- "I do not think ML is the right tool here" is the highest-value sentence
  available to you.
- A negative result, well delivered, leads with the recommendation, shows the
  interval against a pre-agreed bar, and extracts durable findings.
- A document that survives without you: recommendation first, business units,
  what it does *not* do, risks and fallback, how success is measured.
:::
